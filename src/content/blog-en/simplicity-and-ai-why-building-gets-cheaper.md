On the website of Neil West, who worked at Apple for 16 years and published the book "It Just Works" in October 2026, there is a sentence that has stayed with me for weeks: ["AI won't fix complexity. It will amplify it."](https://neilwest.com/) I have not read the book and refer only to this sentence. But it describes what I experienced with FamilyManager.

## A yes costs almost nothing

I work with AI every day, and I would not want to give it up. A new feature, an extra switch, another screen: that is done in hours today, not weeks. That is exactly the danger. The cheaper a yes becomes, the more yeses you collect.

Before FamilyManager 3.0 I clicked through the app and counted. There were 13 screens to the first task, about 90 permanent switches and nine different words for access and security codes. For every question of "Should this be adjustable?" I had tended to answer yes. How much of that is down to AI, I cannot say. But the direction is clear: when building is easy, saying no gets harder than before, because the effort is no longer an excuse.

What the audit found is in [Less is more: what I cut before FamilyManager 3.0](/en/blog/less-is-more-what-i-cut-before-3-0/).

## Faster does not feel faster

There is a second problem. In 2025 the organisation METR had experienced developers work with and without AI. With AI they took 19 percent longer, but believed they were faster. The study is small and uses earlier tools, and the authors say so themselves. The thought behind it stays: when speed feels good, you check less whether it was the right way.

## What I do about it

I have not put AI away. I have put something in front of building that AI does not do for me.

- **Count first, then build.** Screens, switches, words. Once the number is there, cutting gets easy.
- **Four questions before every yes.** Does it help many households? How big are effort and risk? Does it fit a native Apple app? Does it contradict a decision I have already made? The question of whether AI could build it quickly is not on the list.
- **A list for no.** What I deliberately rejected goes on a "do not suggest again" list. It saves me from having the same debate with every new idea, including with myself.
- **Features stay off until they are finished.** I do not switch on half-finished things just because they exist.
- **No empty update for the sake of the date.** If nothing is finished, there is no update.

More on how I weigh wishes in [My best critic is a tester, and still they do not decide what I build](/en/blog/testers-are-customers-not-clients/). How I work with AI is described in [My setup: how I build apps with Claude Code](/en/blog/how-i-build-apps-with-claude-code/).

## Simplify first, then speed up

The idea behind it is simple: AI speeds up what is already there. If it is clear, it gets clear faster. If it is tangled, it gets tangled faster. So my order is now to decide first what the app should be and what it should not, and only then let it be built.

## What I take from this

AI has made building cheap. The work that remains, and weighs more than before, is leaving things out. Anyone working alone on a product has nobody who says no except themselves. For that I need rules I do not have to renegotiate every time.
